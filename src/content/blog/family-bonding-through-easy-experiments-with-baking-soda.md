It's Sunday morning, your 4-year-old is begging for "an explosion," and your 9-year-old has already announced that science is boring. Here's the fix: one box of baking soda, one bottle of vinegar, and a job for every person at the table. The reaction is the same every time, but the roles are what turn it into family time.

> **Quick answer**
>
> - Baking soda plus vinegar makes carbon dioxide gas, and the bubbles are that gas escaping.
> - Give everyone a job: measurer, pourer, predictor, or recorder.
> - Wear goggles, work on a tray or outside, and never seal the mixture in a bottle.
> - Start with a tray of fizzy colors, then let kids change one thing at a time.
> - Keep amounts small and an adult close by, especially with kids under 5.

## What is actually happening in the cup?

Baking soda is sodium bicarbonate, and vinegar is acetic acid. When they meet, they form sodium acetate, water, and carbon dioxide gas, [according to Purdue Extension 4-H](https://www.extension.purdue.edu/4-H/get-involved/discovery_media/5thbakingsoda.pdf). That gas is invisible and heavier than air, and it's what pushes the foam up and makes the bubbles you hear.

You don't need the chemistry words to enjoy it. For kids 8 and up who like formulas, you can write this on a card: sodium bicarbonate + acetic acid gives sodium acetate + water + carbon dioxide. For everyone else, "the gas is trying to escape" is plenty.

## Safety first, in four lines

- Vinegar stings if it gets in eyes, so goggles (swim goggles work) for everyone at the table.
- Never put a lid or cap on a bottle of fizzing mixture; the gas builds pressure and the container can burst.
- Work on a rimmed tray, a baking sheet, or outside on the grass.
- Nothing goes in mouths: not the soda, not the vinegar, not the foam.

Purdue's guide also notes that extra baking soda means extra gas, which raises the risk if anything is closed. Our version keeps every container open, so the fizz has somewhere to go.

## Eight experiments, one for every kind of kid

Each one lists the jobs so you can hand out parts before anyone touches the box. Ages and times are planning guides, not milestones.

![Four family science roles include measurer, pourer, predictor, and recorder with name spaces](/images/blog/family-bonding-through-easy-experiments-with-baking-soda/baking-soda-roles.webp)

### 1. Fizzy Color Tray

**Ages:** 3-10 · **Time:** 15 min · **You need:** a rimmed tray, baking soda, vinegar, food coloring, a pipette or spoon

**Roles:** the measurer spoons soda across the tray, the pourer drips colored vinegar, the predictor guesses which color fizzes most, the recorder draws the result.

Spread a thin layer of baking soda on the tray and set out small cups of vinegar, each with a drop of food coloring. Let your child squeeze or spoon the vinegar onto the powder and watch the color spread and bubble. For a fall version with a seasonal twist, try [Fizzy Pumpkin Potions](/activities/fizzy-pumpkin-potions/).

**Why it fizzes:** the acid in vinegar meets the bicarbonate and releases carbon dioxide, which lifts the foam.

**Ask after:** "Which color made the biggest bubble, and why do you think that was?"

### 2. Balloon Inflator

**Ages:** 5-10 · **Time:** 15 min · **You need:** a plastic bottle, vinegar, baking soda, a balloon, a funnel or paper cone

**Roles:** the measurer funnels 2 teaspoons of baking soda into the balloon, the pourer adds about half a cup of vinegar to the bottle, the predictor guesses how big the balloon gets, the recorder times it.

Stretch the balloon over the bottle neck with the soda inside it, then lift the balloon so the powder falls in. The gas fills the balloon and it stands up on its own.

![Two stages show baking soda entering vinegar and gas inflating a balloon](/images/blog/family-bonding-through-easy-experiments-with-baking-soda/baking-soda-balloon.webp)

**Why it fizzes:** the carbon dioxide has nowhere to go but into the balloon, and the balloon stretches to make room. Never swap the balloon for a cap. Uninflated balloons and popped pieces are a choking risk for young kids, so the adult handles the balloon and picks up any scraps.

**Ask after:** "What would happen if we used twice as much soda?"

### 3. Backyard Volcano

**Ages:** 3-10 · **Time:** 20 min · **You need:** a plastic cup, damp sand or play dough, baking soda, vinegar, a tray

**Roles:** the builder packs the sand or dough into a cone around the cup, the measurer adds soda, the pourer pours vinegar, the predictor calls how high it goes.

Build the cone around the cup with the opening clear at the top, then pour in the soda and add vinegar. Do it on a tray or straight on the grass, and keep faces back a little when it erupts.

**Why it fizzes:** the same gas pushes foam up and out of the cup, and the sand just makes it look like a mountain.

**Ask after:** "Does a taller volcano make a taller eruption?"

### 4. Frozen Fizz Eggs

**Ages:** 3-10 · **Time:** 15 min plus freezing overnight · **You need:** baking soda, water, small plastic eggs or molds, a chunky toy, vinegar, a squeeze bottle

**Roles:** the measurer mixes soda and water into a paste, the builder buries the toy inside, the pourer squeezes vinegar over the frozen egg, the recorder notes how long it takes to melt free.

Mix baking soda with just enough water to make a thick paste, press a large toy into the middle, and freeze in a silicone mold or plastic egg. The next day, set the frozen egg on a tray and let your child squirt vinegar over it until the toy appears. Use chunky toys only, especially for kids who still mouth things.

**Why it fizzes:** the vinegar reacts with the soda in the ice, and the bubbles plus the melting water slowly free the toy.

**Ask after:** "Which melts faster, warm vinegar or cold?"

### 5. Fizzy Sidewalk Paint

**Ages:** 4-10 · **Time:** 20 min · **You need:** baking soda, cornstarch, water, food coloring, squeeze bottles or brushes, a spray bottle of vinegar

**Roles:** the measurer scoops soda and starch, the pourer adds water and color, the painter paints the driveway, the predictor guesses which picture fizzes best.

Mix equal parts baking soda and cornstarch with enough water to make a paintable paste, then divide it into cups with a drop of color each. Paint on the sidewalk or a big sheet of cardboard, then mist with vinegar, spraying low and away from faces, and watch the paint bubble. Rinse the area with the hose afterward.

**Why it fizzes:** the vinegar hits the soda in the dried paint and releases gas right where the color is.

**Ask after:** "What happens if we spray twice in the same spot?"

### 6. Lemon vs Vinegar

**Ages:** 5-10 · **Time:** 15 min · **You need:** three small cups, baking soda, lemon juice, vinegar, water, a spoon

**Roles:** the measurer puts a spoon of soda in each cup, the pourer adds lemon juice, vinegar, and water to separate cups, the predictor ranks them before the pour, the recorder writes down the order.

Line up three cups with the same amount of soda in each, then add a different liquid to each one. Watch which fizzes hardest and talk about why. This is the experiment that quietly introduces the idea of an acid.

**Why it fizzes:** lemon juice and vinegar are both acids, and water isn't, so only two cups bubble.

**Ask after:** "Which one surprised you, and what would you test next?"

### 7. Dancing Raisins

**Ages:** 5-10 · **Time:** 15 min · **You need:** a clear glass, water, baking soda, vinegar, a few raisins

**Roles:** the measurer adds a spoon of soda to the water, the pourer adds a splash of vinegar, the predictor guesses whether the raisins will sink or float, the recorder counts how many times they rise.

Fill a clear glass with water, stir in baking soda, then add vinegar and drop in a few raisins. The raisins ride bubbles up and sink back down, over and over.

**Why it fizzes:** bubbles stick to the raisins and lift them, then pop at the surface and let them fall again.

**Ask after:** "Why do the raisins stop dancing after a while?"

### 8. Cabbage Color Detective

**Ages:** 7-10 · **Time:** 25 min · **You need:** red cabbage, hot water, a strainer, small cups, baking soda, vinegar, a spoon

**Roles:** the adult makes the cabbage water, the measurer pours it into cups, the pourer adds soda to one and vinegar to another, the recorder draws the colors.

An adult chops a few red cabbage leaves, covers them with hot water, and lets them steep until the water is purple, then strains it. Kids add a spoon of baking soda to one cup and vinegar to another and watch the color change. The hot water stays with the adult the whole time.

**Why it changes color:** cabbage water reacts differently to acids and bases, so it shifts from purple to pink or blue-green.

**Ask after:** "What color tells you something is an acid?"

## Make it a family ritual

Pick one day a week and call it Science Sunday. Do one reaction, then change exactly one thing next time: more soda, colder vinegar, a different liquid. Rotate the four jobs each week so the same child isn't always the measurer.

## Common questions

### Is baking soda and vinegar safe for toddlers?

The experiments here start at age 3. If a younger child joins, you do the pouring and they drip a little colored vinegar with a spoon or dropper. Vinegar stings eyes, so goggles help, and nothing goes in mouths. Keep amounts small and containers open.

### Why does baking soda fizz with vinegar?

Baking soda is a base and vinegar is an acid, and when they meet they form sodium acetate, water, and carbon dioxide gas. The gas is invisible and heavier than air, and the bubbles and foam are it escaping.

### How do you clean up a baking soda volcano?

Let the foam settle, then wipe it up with a damp cloth or paper towels and rinse the tray. Outdoors, hose the area down so the residue doesn't dry into a crust. Don't rinse sand or play dough down the sink.

### What else reacts with baking soda?

Lemon juice, cream of tartar, and buttermilk are all acids that will fizz with baking soda, which is why recipes pair them. Try lemon juice next to vinegar and let your kids rank which one bubbles more.
