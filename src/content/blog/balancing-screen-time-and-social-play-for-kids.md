Balancing screen time and social play starts with protecting sleep, movement, meals, and opportunities to play with other people. Put those into your day first, then choose manageable screen windows with clear endings. You don't need to account for every second, but you do need boundaries that leave room for the rest of childhood.

> **Quick answer**
>
> - Plan sleep, movement, meals, and social play before recreational screens.
> - Use age guidance alongside content, context, and your child's needs.
> - Keep a short list of games you can offer when screens stop.
> - Make some viewing or gaming a shared activity, with a planned ending.
> - Review the plan if screens repeatedly interfere with sleep, friends, or family life.

It's Saturday, and your 5- and 7-year-old are sitting side by side, each watching a tablet. Nobody is doing anything wrong, but the afternoon you'd pictured together is disappearing. Start with one invitation: “Let's put these on the shelf and build somewhere to read.”

**This is general information, not medical advice. If something worries you, talk to your pediatrician.**

## Is there a right number of hours?

There isn't one daily allowance that suits every child and every kind of screen use. A video call, school assignment, and autoplay feed aren't interchangeable, even though each uses a screen.

The [WHO's guidance for children under 5](https://www.who.int/news/item/24-04-2019-to-grow-up-healthy-children-need-to-sit-less-and-play-more) gives age-based limits for sedentary screen time. The [AAP's policy released in January 2026](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/doi/10.1542/peds.2025-075320/206129/Digital-Ecosystems-Children-and-Adolescents-) takes a broader approach, emphasizing quality, relationships, and what media displaces.

That doesn't mean AAP abandoned time boundaries. It gives flexible examples when families want numbers, rather than one universal prescription.

| Age | WHO under-5 guidance | AAP's current approach |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Under 1 | Screen time not recommended | Prioritize real-world interaction; infants under 18 months struggle to transfer screen information to real life |
| Age 1 | Sedentary screen time not recommended | Adult interaction and developmental needs come first |
| Age 2 | No more than 1 hour of sedentary screen time daily; less is better | Individualize boundaries; its example range for toddlers and preschoolers is under 1 hour daily |
| Ages 3–4 | No more than 1 hour daily; less is better | Consider quality, co-use, and activities being displaced, alongside time boundaries |
| Ages 5–10 | Outside this WHO guideline's age range | Individualize recreational limits; for school-age children, AAP gives examples of 1–2 hours or more, depending on circumstances |

Sources: [WHO age recommendations](https://www.who.int/news/item/24-04-2019-to-grow-up-healthy-children-need-to-sit-less-and-play-more) and [AAP's policy, including “Set time boundaries”](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/doi/10.1542/peds.2025-075320/206129/Digital-Ecosystems-Children-and-Adolescents-). These are different frameworks, not permission to choose whichever number allows the longest session.

For ages 3–4, [WHO also recommends](https://www.who.int/news/item/24-04-2019-to-grow-up-healthy-children-need-to-sit-less-and-play-more) at least 180 minutes of varied movement across the day, including at least 60 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous activity, and 10–13 hours of sleep. Movement can happen in ordinary play; it doesn't require a three-hour exercise class.

## Why play with other people matters

The [AAP's Power of Play report](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/news/Pages/healthier-children-play-according-to-the-aap.aspx) describes play as supporting language, relationships, executive functioning, and coping with stress. Executive functioning includes skills such as holding a rule in mind and stopping an impulse.

You can see those skills when children decide whose turn comes next or change a game so everyone can join. Handling disappointment doesn't mean never crying after a loss; it can mean getting help, calming down, and trying again.

Social play can involve a sibling, caregiver, classmate, or neighbor. A child doesn't need a packed playdate calendar, and a quiet child shouldn't have to perform sociability all afternoon.

## What screens can crowd out

### Background television and everyday talk

[Christakis and colleagues' 2009 study](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapediatrics/fullarticle/381618) used recordings from 329 children ages 2–48 months. More audible television was associated with fewer adult words, child vocalizations, and conversational exchanges.

It was an observational study, not proof that a particular program caused a language problem. An easy household experiment is to switch off the television when nobody is actively watching and notice whether conversation feels easier.

### Early screen use and later development

[A Japanese study of 7,097 mother-child pairs](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapediatrics/fullarticle/2808593) linked greater screen time at age 1 with communication and problem-solving delays at ages 2 and 4. Some associations with fine motor and personal-social skills appeared at age 2 but weren't confirmed at age 4.

The findings don't establish cause, and they don't tell us what a short shared session means for a school-age child. The authors discuss possible reverse causation, including children with developmental difficulties spending more time on screens.

### Interruptions from parents' devices

[McDaniel and Radesky's longitudinal study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6185759/) recruited 183 couples and analyzed data from 337 parents after exclusions. Parent-reported technology interruptions, parenting stress, and children's behavior were linked in patterns that could work in both directions.

The sample was small and relied on surveys. This isn't evidence that answering a work message harms your child; it suggests looking at repeated interruptions during time you're trying to spend together.

## Plan the day first, then fit screens in

Sketch an ordinary school day and a weekend day together. Add bedtime, getting ready, school or preschool, meals, movement, and a realistic chance to connect with someone before choosing recreational screen windows.

A weekday might have a snack and a shared game after school, then a planned episode while you finish dinner. On the weekend, you might protect a visit with cousins and choose a family movie later. These are planning examples, not recommended schedules or quotas.

Use the [weekly screen and play plan](/downloads/screen-play-weekly-plan.pdf) to write down what you'll try. Its rows separate shared and solo screen use so you can notice the difference, without making shared viewing unlimited.

For broader household rules, the [AAP Family Media Plan](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/fmp/Pages/MediaPlan.aspx) helps organize the conversation. Leave some breathing room: a sick day, a long trip, or a difficult workweek may need a different plan.

## Eight easy swaps from screens to social play

Offer a specific first action instead of “Go play.” Keep the setup small enough that you can follow through, and let children adapt the game.

### Rainy afternoon: Freeze Dance

Try [Freeze Dance](/activities/freeze-dance/) with the music playing from a device whose screen is put away. Take turns choosing a silly freeze pose, and clear a safe space before moving.

### Friends arrive: Pass the Parcel

Set out [Pass the Parcel](/activities/pass-the-parcel/) before everyone settles around separate screens. Use a shared silly prompt or group celebration so the game doesn't depend on buying a pile of prizes.

### After preschool: Follow the Leader

Your 4-year-old may be too tired for another set of instructions. Offer a gentle version of [Follow the Leader](/activities/follow-the-leader/): “Show me how a sleepy bear walks,” then let them lead.

### Dinner preparation: Story Chain

Start [Story Chain](/activities/story-chain/) from a safe spot away from the stove: “A tiny dog found a giant sandwich.” Each person adds a sentence; younger children can contribute a sound or choose what happens next.

### Older and younger siblings: Shadow Puppet Theater

In [Shadow Puppet Theater](/activities/shadow-puppet-theater/), one child can make shapes while another narrates. Give each a role and keep the light source safely placed rather than asking the older child to manage everything.

### Long drive: A traveling story

Use the same story game in the car, taking turns inventing where a character goes next. Everyone stays buckled, and the driver doesn't have to participate or look at anything.

### Waiting room: Quiet guessing

Borrow the puppet game's idea without equipment: shape a hand into an animal and let your child guess. Keep it quiet and contained; if your child is sick or distressed, comfort matters more than turning the wait into practice.

### Before bed: Blanket-fort reading

Try a [blanket-fort reading den](/activities/blanket-fort-reading-den/) with light blankets and stable, low furniture. Share a book, then return to your normal sleep space; the fort is a supervised play spot, not a new sleeping setup.

## Make screen time more social

Ask a question during a show, take turns solving a puzzle, or let your child explain what they're building. Sitting nearby can help, but connection comes from responding to each other, not simply sharing the couch.

The [AAP's parent guide](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/Media/Pages/helping-kids-thrive-in-a-digital-world-AAP-policy-explained.aspx) suggests co-viewing and considering a shared family tablet rather than personal tablets. A video call with Grandma can also involve real back-and-forth: show her a drawing and wait for her response.

For a short shared activity, try [our learning games](/learn/) together and agree on an ending before you start. For gaming with online contacts, use our [online friendship safety guide](/blog/10-tips-to-help-kids-navigate-online-friendships-safely/) to discuss privacy, settings, and getting adult help.

## Turning screens off without a meltdown

You can't guarantee a cheerful ending, but you can make it more predictable. Before starting, say, “One episode, then we'll put the tablet away and choose a story.”

Turn off autoplay where available, give a warning, and aim for an agreed stopping point such as the end of a level. A game with no workable pause may be a poor fit for the few minutes before dinner.

When your 8-year-old asks to keep playing with friends, try “Tell them this is your last round. We'll plan another time tomorrow.” If the round keeps extending, agree on a firm finish time before the next session.

Have the next action ready: “You can be upset. The tablet is finished, and I'm here to help.” You can hold a limit without treating distress as misbehavior or automatically using screens as a reward for everything else.

## Your phone counts too

Choose one routine where adults put phones aside along with the kids, such as the meal you've marked on the weekly plan. If you need to respond, explain briefly and return: “I'm answering the school, then I'm back.”

A shared charging place outside bedrooms can make the boundary visible. The [AAP's parent guidance](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/Media/Pages/helping-kids-thrive-in-a-digital-world-AAP-policy-explained.aspx) also recommends parents model breaks at meals and bedtime.

You don't have to be available every moment. The goal is a dependable chance to reconnect, not a rule that makes work, accessibility, or caregiving needs harder to manage.

## When to ask for help

Talk with your pediatrician if screens repeatedly interfere with sleep, your child loses interest in friends or other activities, or stopping becomes persistently distressing. A single difficult transition doesn't establish a diagnosis.

Bring examples of what your child uses, when problems happen, and what you've tried. The [AAP's current policy](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/doi/10.1542/peds.2025-075320/206129/Digital-Ecosystems-Children-and-Adolescents-) encourages support that looks at the child and family situation, including underlying difficulties, rather than blaming parents.

## Common questions

### How much screen time should a 5-year-old have?

There isn't one universal number for every 5-year-old. Consider school demands, content, shared versus solo use, and whether sleep and play fit comfortably; AAP supports tailored time boundaries, and WHO's under-5 guideline doesn't cover children who have turned 5.

### Is screen time bad for social skills?

It depends on the experience and what it replaces. Research reports associations that vary by age and outcome; a video call or shared game differs from long solo sessions, and screens don't replace opportunities to practice playing with people offline.

### Do video calls with grandparents count as screen time?

They use a screen, but interactive conversation differs from passive viewing. You can record calls under “screen together” on the plan while still protecting sleep, movement, and other activities.

### What if my child only wants to play video games with friends?

Ask what they enjoy about that time, then protect a manageable gaming window alongside other ways to connect. Invite an existing friend into an easy offline activity, and ask for help if your child consistently withdraws from everything else or gaming disrupts daily life.
