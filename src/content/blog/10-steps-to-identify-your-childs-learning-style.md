To figure out how your child learns best, watch what helps them understand something, then check what they remember later. You don't need a quiz that labels them a visual, auditory, or hands-on learner. Try the 10 steps below over two ordinary weeks and look for useful patterns, not a permanent type.

> **Quick answer**
>
> - Preferences are real, but matching lessons to a learning-style label isn't well supported.
> - Choose a clear goal and use examples that fit the subject.
> - Ask your child to explain or show what they remember.
> - Return to the idea on another day instead of drilling it all at once.
> - Record what helped and discuss lasting difficulties with the teacher.

Imagine your 6-year-old's quiz result says “visual learner,” so you buy picture cards. A week later, the letters still aren't sticking. That doesn't mean you chose the wrong pictures or your child failed to use their strengths; the label may never have been useful for planning instruction.

## What the research says about learning styles

The [Pashler and colleagues review, published in the journal's 2008 volume](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/j.1539-6053.2009.01038.x), found no adequate evidence base for using learning-style assessments in general education. Its central question was specific: do people learn better when instruction matches an assigned style? Having a preference isn't enough to establish that benefit.

If you've used these labels, you're in plenty of company. A [2020 review by Newton and Salvi](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2020.602451/full) covered 37 studies involving 15,405 educators in 18 countries and found a weighted 89.1% reported belief in matching teaching to styles.

That figure describes the surveyed educators, not every teacher. The authors also noted limitations, including convenience samples and questions that might blur preferences with the matching claim.

Children do differ in knowledge, interests, language, sensory access, and support needs. Those differences matter. The practical response is to find out what a particular task requires and what help makes it accessible, rather than restricting a child to one format.

## 1. Notice what they choose without directions

Watch a few minutes of free play. Does your 4-year-old build animal homes, retell a story, or sort toy cars? Use that interest as a way into something you want to explore together.

For a child who loves cars, park three cars and add two more to talk about five. The interest gets you started; it doesn't establish a “car learner” type or require every future math activity to involve vehicles.

Write an observation rather than a judgment: “Kept rebuilding the ramp after cars fell off.” That gives you an idea to revisit without predicting their abilities from one afternoon.

## 2. Notice how long focus lasts in this situation

Try a short task and quietly note when your child needs a change. Record the task and the setting alongside the time; six minutes on a puzzle after snack isn't directly comparable to six minutes copying letters before bed.

[CDC lists attention for 5–10 minutes during an activity as a milestone at age 5](https://www.cdc.gov/act-early/milestones/5-years.html), excluding screen time. It's a developmental reference, not a timer that every child must meet for every assignment.

If your 5-year-old starts sliding under the table, offer a pause or a smaller task. Try “Let's do this one together, then stretch,” instead of using the observation to assign an attention label.

## 3. Teach one idea with pictures, words, and action

Choose something small, such as why three plus two makes five. Draw three dots and two dots, explain the groups in plain language, and move five blocks together.

Keep the examples connected and brief. More formats aren't automatically better if they're busy or unrelated; the purpose is to make the same idea clear. A picture helps show shape, while listening matters when learning a new sound.

Come back a week later and ask your child to solve a similar problem. This informal check helps you plan what to do next; it isn't a controlled experiment that identifies their best sensory channel.

## 4. Ask them to teach it back

Close the book or put the example aside. Ask, “Can you show the teddy how we made five?” Your child can explain, draw, point, or move objects, depending on what the goal requires.

This is a playful form of retrieval practice: trying to bring information back to mind. [The Learning Scientists explain retrieval and feedback](https://www.learningscientists.org/faq) as ways to support remembering.

When the answer is incomplete, give a small cue and then the missing information. “We started with three. What happened next?” is more helpful than repeating the question until your child feels trapped.

## 5. Return to it on another day

Spend a few minutes on the idea, then revisit it after a gap. For example, read a new word Monday, look for it in a book Thursday, and ask about it again the following week.

That's a home version of spaced practice, one of the strategies described by [The Learning Scientists](https://www.learningscientists.org/faq). The exact gap can vary; you don't need an elaborate calendar for preschool practice.

Your 7-year-old may recognize a word while looking at yesterday's worksheet but forget it in a different book. Use the new setting as another chance to practice, with help as needed, rather than evidence that the earlier effort was wasted.

## 6. Watch where the task breaks down

Instead of recording “hates writing,” notice the difficult part. Can your child tell you a story but struggle to hold the pencil? Do they understand a spoken direction but lose track when it has several parts?

Change one demand at a time. Let them tell the story while you write, or give the first direction and wait before adding another. You're checking what support helps, not diagnosing a condition.

Record the change in the log: “Told the whole story when I wrote the words.” Share repeated patterns with the teacher, especially when a difficulty continues across activities.

## 7. Leave room for a slower answer

After asking a question, pause before explaining it again. Some children start talking while they think; others need a quiet moment to organize an answer.

With an 8-year-old solving a subtraction problem, try “Take your time. Show me when you're ready.” Avoid treating the quickest response as the most knowledgeable one.

If waiting doesn't help, offer a concrete prompt or work through an example. Pace is one piece of the observation, and speed alone doesn't tell you what your child understands.

## 8. Check the basics around the task

Notice whether the activity happens after a short night, a missed snack, or a noisy car ride. Try a calmer setting or a different time before deciding the material needs a new teaching method.

If you notice trouble seeing a page, hearing directions, pain, or persistent fatigue, raise it with your child's healthcare professional. Keep routine vision and hearing discussions on the list at well-child visits.

Don't use a home learning log to rule out a health concern. Its job is to describe what you're seeing so you can ask a better question.

## 9. Compare notes with the teacher

Bring two or three examples rather than asking, “What's their learning style?” Try “They can explain the story aloud, but writing one sentence takes a lot of help. What do you see at school?”

Ask what instruction the class is using and what a reasonable next goal looks like. A shared plan is easier to follow than adding a separate stack of practice pages at home.

Also ask what goes well at school. You might learn that your child works comfortably with a peer, needs directions repeated, or understands more when an adult models the first example.

## 10. Keep a two-week observation log

Use the [free observation log](/downloads/learning-observation-log.pdf) for a short note each day you try an activity. Record the task, minutes, what helped, and where your child got stuck.

Include one later check of something you practiced before. “Remembered two story events three days later” says more about learning than “liked the story video.”

After two weeks, choose one useful adjustment, such as shorter directions or another brief review later in the week. You can stop logging once it has answered your question; this isn't homework for parents.

## When the issue needs more than a different activity

Talk with the teacher when difficulty keeps returning, your child regularly avoids reading or number work, or ordinary assignments require much more help than expected. Ask what support is available and when to review whether it's helping.

You don't have to spend months trying every format before raising a concern. Contact your pediatrician as well if you're worried about development, hearing, vision, or your child's distress.

A learning-style label can't explain persistent difficulty. Keep the conversation focused on the specific skill, the instruction already tried, and the next source of support.

## What to try at home instead of a style quiz

Use [Story Chain](/activities/story-chain/) to recall what happened before adding a new event. Play [Guess the Sound](/activities/guess-the-sound/) for listening and describing, without telling a child they must be an auditory learner to enjoy it.

A [picture-clue treasure hunt](/activities/picture-clue-treasure-hunt/) combines looking, talking, and moving. [Five Things](/activities/five-things/) gives you a quick way to notice and describe details together.

For ages 5–8, explore the [Kidtivity Lab learning games](/learn/) alongside your child. Choose one small goal, let them try it, and ask what they remember later; finishing every activity isn't necessary.

## Common questions

### Are learning styles real?

People have preferences and different abilities, but the claim that matching teaching to a visual, auditory, or kinesthetic label improves learning lacks adequate support. Focus on the subject and on what your child understands and remembers.

### How do I know if my child is a visual learner?

You can notice that they enjoy diagrams or pictures without assigning a fixed type. Use visuals when they clarify the content, and keep spoken explanations, reading, and practical experience available too.

### What are the four VARK learning styles?

VARK stands for visual, auditory, read/write, and kinesthetic. Those are categories in a popular model, not a diagnosis or a requirement for choosing how to teach your child.

### Can a child's learning style change?

Preferences can vary with the topic, experience, and situation. Rather than retesting a label, keep adjusting the task and checking whether the child can use what they've learned.
